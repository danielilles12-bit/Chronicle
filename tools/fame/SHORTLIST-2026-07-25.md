# Content shortlist — 25 July 2026

**Out of 4,292 harvested candidates, 29 are worth adding to the game. That is seven in every thousand.**

This is the honest answer to the worry that we harvested far too much. We did. The harvest was worth doing — it cost nothing but machine time and it proves where the ceiling is — but it is not a shopping list of four thousand items. It is a shopping list of 29, and even those are here to widen what the game talks about, not to make the rotation work. The rotation already works: Face Value has 426 items against a need of 210, Lifeline 541, Relic 364.

### How something earned a place

Three tests, and an item had to pass all three.

1. **Is it a thing at all?** For objects: specific, real, photographable, and at least fifty years old. This is what removes "passenger car", the Flying Dutchman and an F-35.
2. **Would anyone recognise it?** It has to score at least as well as the bottom quarter of what already airs — a bar read off the live game rather than invented.
3. **Is it history?** The same test again, but on the history-lover score rather than raw web traffic. This is the test that removes Bing Crosby, Shirley Temple and Lucille Ball, all of whom sail through test 2.

Test 3 is the new one, and it is what changed the answer at the top of the list. Ranked on public popularity alone, the top of the shortlist read Concorde, Volkswagen Beetle, International Space Station, passenger car, Bing Crosby, an F-35 fighter, Joan Crawford, Shirley Temple, Fred Astaire, Dean Martin. Popularity measures who gets looked up, and that means recent. History is a different question, and now it is asked separately.

Everything below is sorted best-first, both within each section and between them.

## Face Value and Lifeline — 19 people

### Music

- **Miles Davis** — fame 94.7, salience 93.2. The most photographed face in jazz, and the pool has almost no twentieth-century musicians at all.
- **Ravi Shankar** — fame 92.7, salience 89.9. The sitar player who took Indian classical music worldwide; a 13,200 km Varanasi-to-San-Diego life makes a strong Lifeline round.
- **Duke Ellington** — fame 89.4, salience 87.8. Jazz's great composer-bandleader — pairs with Miles Davis without repeating him.
- **Buddy Holly** — fame 92.5, salience 84.8. Glasses, Fender, and the 1959 plane crash; instantly readable in a portrait, which is what Face Value needs.

### Politics and power

- **Sheikh Mujibur Rahman** — fame 89.6, salience 95.5. Founding leader of Bangladesh, assassinated in 1975. The single best gap-filler here: South Asian, twentieth-century, and a founder rather than a monarch.
- **Caracalla** — fame 87.5, salience 88.4. Roman emperor, and the Baths of Caracalla are already a Relic round — the two make a satisfying pair. Caveat: the pool is already 32% statesmen.
- **Joseph McCarthy** — fame 89.2, salience 86.5. McCarthyism named itself after him; twentieth-century American politics is under-covered and he is the most recognisable way in.
- **Nelson Aldrich Rockefeller** — fame 90.8, salience 82.1. US Vice-President and art collector. The weakest person on the list — he clears both bars, but only just on salience.

### Work, money and reform

- **Andrew Carnegie** — fame 92.4, salience 90.4. Steel magnate turned library-builder: the pool is thin on business, work and philanthropy, and his Dunfermline-to-Massachusetts life is a 5,000 km Lifeline.
- **Emma Goldman** — fame 88.0, salience 92.3. Anarchist, deported from America; born in the Russian Empire, died in Toronto — 6,900 km, and a non-ruling woman with a genuinely international life.

### Thinkers, writers and scientists

- **Sun Tzu** — fame 90.2, salience 91.9. The Art of War — an In Our Time subject, top-rated by Wikipedia's historians, and one of very few East Asian figures anywhere in the harvest that clears the bar.
- **Pliny the Elder** — fame 90.0, salience 87.7. Wrote the first encyclopaedia and died walking towards Vesuvius — an In Our Time subject and a natural pairing with Pompeii, already in the game.
- **Euripides** — fame 85.6, salience 91.8. The third of the Athenian tragedians; the pool has the buildings of classical Greece and few of its people.
- **William James** — fame 87.5, salience 87.9. Founded American psychology; an In Our Time subject. Science and medicine are among the thinnest subjects in the pool.
- **John Dewey** — fame 88.0, salience 86.8. The philosopher who rebuilt how schools teach — education is a subject the pool never touches.
- **Jonathan Swift** — fame 89.0, salience 85.0. Gulliver's Travels; an In Our Time subject. Face Value only — he was born and died in Dublin, so his Lifeline journey is zero kilometres.

### Faith and legend — Face Value only

- **Saint George** — fame 91.6, salience 87.1. Patron saint of England, Georgia and much else, and a fixture of European painting. Do not use him on Lifeline: his birth and death points are tradition, not record.
- **Thomas the Apostle** — fame 89.5, salience 88.3. The apostle who, by tradition, reached India. Same caveat: a 5,000 km Galilee-to-Mylapore journey is a lovely map round and an invented one.

### Sport

- **Jackie Robinson** — fame 88.9, salience 86.7. Broke baseball's colour bar in 1947. Sport is one of the four thinnest subjects in the pool and he is the only sportsman in the whole harvest to clear both bars.

## Relic — 10 objects

### Medals and orders

- **Order of the British Empire** — fame 96.7, salience 93.9. The most recognised honour in the English-speaking world, instituted 1917.
- **Medal of Honor** — fame 93.3, salience 94.8. The United States' highest military decoration, 1862 — visually distinctive and genuinely portable.
- **Legion of Honour** — fame 87.9, salience 90.8. Napoleon's order of merit, 1802, and the model most modern honours copied.
- **Victoria Cross** — fame 87.1, salience 90.7. Cast, by tradition, from captured Russian guns; 1856.
- **Padma Shri** — fame 90.9, salience 82.0. India's civilian honour, 1954 — the only non-Western decoration to clear the bar.

### Machines and vehicles

- **Volkswagen Beetle** — fame 98.2, salience 90.4. The 1930s People's Car that became the 1960s Beetle — a rare object that carries both social history and instant recognition.
- **Sputnik 1** — fame 88.5, salience 97.5. Highest salience of anything on this list. A 58 cm polished sphere from 1957 that started the space age — portable, photogenic, and unmistakably historical.
- **Concorde** — fame 99.2, salience 82.0. Highest public recognition of anything on this list, retired in 2003, and it lands exactly on the fifty-year line.

### Ships

- **RMS Lusitania** — fame 85.9, salience 89.6. Torpedoed in 1915 and one of the reasons America entered the First World War.
- **USS Enterprise** — fame 87.0, salience 80.5. The first nuclear-powered aircraft carrier (1962, retired 2017). The weakest object here: no WikiProject rates its historical importance at all, so its score leans on traffic. Note the answer would need to name the ship — 'USS Enterprise' alone is ambiguous.

A warning about the medals: five of the ten objects are decorations. They are genuinely good Relic material — small, portable, instantly readable, and the pool has almost nothing portable — but five of them in one pool would feel like a theme night. Two or three is probably the right number.

The fifty-year rule is not a hunch. A space station still in orbit and a fighter still in production are news with a Wikipedia rating, not history. The same measurement says the opposite about people, so no such rule was applied to them: 82 of the 541 Lifeline figures died in 1976 or later.

## The four gaps: what this fixes and what it does not

**Non-ruler people — fixed, and this is the real prize.** Two of the 19 are rulers (a Roman emperor and the founder of Bangladesh) and two more are elected American politicians. The other fifteen are four musicians, six writers and thinkers, a steel magnate, an anarchist, a baseball player and two saints. The pool it is joining is 32% statesmen.

**Portable objects — partly fixed.** Six of the ten objects are things you could carry: Sputnik 1 and five decorations. The pool is overwhelmingly buildings and monuments, so that is real progress, but it is six items, not sixty.

**Sub-Saharan Africa — not fixed.** Only 46 African candidates were harvested and only four have ever been measured. None clears the bar. Chinua Achebe misses by 0.7 and Miriam Makeba by 3.9, both with strong history scores — they are in the "just below the line" list above, and they are the whole answer the harvest has to offer here.

**Oceania — not fixed at all.** 268 candidates were harvested; five have ever been measured; the best of them, Bruce McLaren, scores 73.5 against a bar of 84.6. There is nothing in this harvest for Oceania. If that gap matters, it needs deliberate hand-picking, not another sweep.

## The honest verdict

A few dozen good items, not four thousand. The harvest's real value was diagnostic: it proved that the pools are not short of material, and that casting a wider net mostly catches things less recognisable than what already airs. 1,983 of the 2,520 people harvested have never been measured at all (every object has been), and they are demonstrably the tail — they have a Wikipedia page in a median of 26 languages against 63 for the people we did measure. Measuring the remaining 1,983 would be a large job for a small return; if it is ever done, the 421 of them with pages in 40 or more languages are the only part worth doing.

If only ten of these 29 ever get made into rounds, pick them for what they let the game talk about that it cannot today: a jazz trumpeter, a sitar player, a philosopher of education, a baseball player who broke a colour bar, a Bangladeshi founder, a steel magnate who gave it all away, an anarchist, a satellite, a supersonic airliner and a medal.
